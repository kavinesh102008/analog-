1. Introduction and learning objectives

A voltage-series feedback amplifier is a negative-feedback amplifier in which a fraction of the output voltage is sampled across the output terminals and fed back in series with the input signal. It is also called a series–shunt feedback amplifier: series mixing occurs at the input and shunt (voltage) sampling occurs at the output.

The feedback voltage opposes the applied signal. This reduces the overall gain but makes the gain more stable, increases input resistance and decreases output resistance. It is widely associated with voltage amplifiers and the non-inverting op-amp configuration.

Core result: For a stable negative-feedback loop, the closed-loop voltage gain is Af = A / (1 + Aβ) where A is the open-loop gain and β is the feedback factor.

Learning objectives

  • Identify series mixing and voltage sampling in the topology.
  • Derive closed-loop gain and explain loop gain Aβ.
  • Predict the effect of feedback on input/output resistance.
  • Analyse a non-inverting op-amp as a practical example.
  • Solve numerical problems and interpret frequency response and stability.

Prerequisites

Voltage gain, Ohm’s law, Kirchhoff’s laws, voltage dividers, ideal op-amp rules, complex impedance and basic Bode plots.

3. Working principle

  1. Apply input: The source provides Vs to the input summing arrangement.
  2. Amplify: The amplifier produces output Vo = A Vi.
  3. Sample voltage: The output network senses the output voltage and returns Vf = βVo.
  4. Series subtract: The feedback voltage opposes the source, so Vi = Vs − Vf.
  5. Reach equilibrium: The loop settles where the amplifier output and feedback satisfy both equations, provided the loop is stable and the amplifier is not saturated.
Vo = A(Vs − βVo)   ⇒   Vo/Vs = A/(1 + Aβ)

Why the name voltage-series?

  • Voltage sampling: The feedback network is connected across the output, so it senses output voltage (shunt sampling).
  • Series mixing: The feedback voltage is inserted in series opposition with the source at the input.

Important operating conditions

  • Feedback must be negative at the frequency of interest.
  • The amplifier should operate in its linear region, not in saturation or current limiting.
  • For the simple gain formula, A and β are treated as scalar gains at the frequency considered.
  • For frequency-dependent circuits, A(s) and β(s) are complex functions and stability must be checked.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages
  • Stable and predictable closed-loop gain.
  • Higher input resistance.
  • Lower output resistance.
  • Reduced nonlinear distortion and sensitivity.
  • Often wider usable bandwidth for a dominant-pole amplifier.
Limitations
  • Closed-loop gain is lower than open-loop gain.
  • Available output swing and slew rate remain limited.
  • Feedback can cause instability if phase shift makes it effectively positive.
  • Noise, offset and bias currents are not eliminated.
  • Finite gain-bandwidth and component tolerances affect accuracy.
Common misconception: Negative feedback does not always mean the output waveform is inverted by 180°. “Negative” describes the sign of the returned signal at the summing point, not necessarily the phase relation between source and output.

4. Mathematical equations and derivation

Closed-loop voltage gain

Let A = Vo/Vi, β = Vf/Vo, and Vi = Vs − Vf.

Vo = A Vi = A(Vs − βVo)
Vo(1 + Aβ) = AVs   ⇒   Af = A/(1 + Aβ)

A, β and Af are dimensionless voltage ratios (V/V). Aβ is the dimensionless loop gain.

Large-loop-gain approximation

If |Aβ| ≫ 1 and the loop is stable:

Af ≈ 1/β

The gain is then mainly set by the feedback network, rather than the exact open-loop gain.

Gain desensitivity

D = 1 + Aβ

For small changes in A, the fractional closed-loop gain change is approximately the open-loop fractional change divided by 1 + Aβ. More exactly, the sensitivity is:

SAAf = (∂Af/∂A)(A/Af) = 1/(1 + Aβ)

Input and output resistance

For voltage-series feedback, series mixing raises input resistance and voltage (shunt) sampling lowers output resistance:

Rif = Ri(1 + Aβ)    ;    Rof = Ro/(1 + Aβ)

These are idealised low-frequency results for a unilateral amplifier model with negative feedback. Actual impedances depend on frequency, loading and circuit topology.

Non-inverting op-amp feedback factor

For Rf from output to the inverting node and R1 from that node to ground, the divider gives:

β = R1/(R1 + Rf)
Af = 1/β = 1 + Rf/R1   (ideal op-amp approximation)

The exact finite-open-loop-gain result is Af = A/[1 + Aβ]. The familiar resistor gain assumes sufficiently large open-loop gain and operation within bandwidth and output limits.

Frequency response and bandwidth

For a single-dominant-pole voltage amplifier with approximately constant β, feedback trades gain for bandwidth. Under the usual gain-bandwidth approximation:

Af × BWf ≈ A × BW ≈ GBW

This approximation is not universal; it is most useful for a dominant-pole op-amp in its linear region. Real devices have finite phase margin, additional poles/zeros and slew-rate limits.

Decibel conversion

Av,dB = 20 log10|Av|

Use 20 log for voltage gain when input/output impedances are appropriately comparable or when simply expressing a voltage ratio. For power ratios use 10 log10(Po/Pi).

5. Solved numerical problems

Example 1 — Basic closed-loop gain

Given: A = 1000, β = 0.05. Find Af.

Formula: Af = A/(1 + Aβ).

Aβ = 1000 × 0.05 = 50
Af = 1000/(1 + 50) = 1000/51 = 19.61 V/V

Interpretation: Feedback reduces the gain from 1000 to about 19.61 while improving gain stability.

Example 2 — Design a non-inverting amplifier

Given: Desired ideal gain = 11, choose R1 = 10 kΩ. Find Rf.

Formula: Af = 1 + Rf/R1.

11 = 1 + Rf/10 kΩ
Rf = 10 × 10 kΩ = 100 kΩ

Interpretation: Select standard 100 kΩ and 10 kΩ resistors. The theoretical gain is 11 V/V, subject to op-amp limits and resistor tolerances.

Example 3 — GATE-style: impedance change

Given: Ri = 2 kΩ, Ro = 10 kΩ and loop gain Aβ = 49. Find Rif and Rof.

1 + Aβ = 50
Rif = 2 kΩ × 50 = 100 kΩ
Rof = 10 kΩ/50 = 200 Ω

Interpretation: Input resistance increases by 50 times, while output resistance decreases by 50 times.

Example 4 — Finite gain accuracy

Given: A = 2000, β = 0.1, Vs = 0.2 V. Find Vo and compare with the ideal high-loop-gain value.

Af = 2000/(1 + 200) = 2000/201 = 9.9502
Vo = 9.9502 × 0.2 = 1.990 V

Ideal approximation: 1/β = 10, so Vo,ideal = 2.000 V. The finite-gain result is slightly lower.

Shortcut: When Aβ is very large, use Af ≈ 1/β for a quick estimate. Use A/(1 + Aβ) when exact finite-gain accuracy is required.

For a non-inverting voltage-series amplifier, the output is normally in phase with the source at frequencies well inside the closed-loop passband. Its amplitude is larger by the closed-loop gain. The returned feedback voltage is in the opposing sense at the summing node.

Suggested Multisim procedure

  1. Build a non-inverting op-amp circuit with an appropriate op-amp model and supply rails.
  2. Connect Rf from output to the inverting input and R1 from the inverting input to ground.
  3. Apply a small sine input, for example 100 mV peak at 1 kHz, ensuring the output will not clip.
  4. Use an oscilloscope to display input and output simultaneously.
  5. Measure Vin and Vout; calculate gain = Vout/Vin.
  6. Run AC analysis to inspect gain and phase versus frequency; compare the measured passband gain with 1 + Rf/R1.

8. Core electronics company interview preparation

1. What is voltage-series feedback?

It is negative feedback in which output voltage is sampled across the load and a proportional feedback voltage is mixed in series opposition with the input. It is also called series–shunt feedback.

2. Why is it called series–shunt feedback?

At the input, the feedback signal is combined in series with the source. At the output, the network is connected across the output terminals to sample voltage.

3. Derive the closed-loop gain.

Vi = Vs − βVo and Vo = AVi. Substitution gives Vo(1 + Aβ) = AVs, hence Af = A/(1 + Aβ).

4. What happens to input and output resistance?

For voltage-series feedback, input resistance increases by approximately 1 + Aβ and output resistance decreases by the same factor, under the standard feedback model.

5. What is loop gain?

Loop gain is T = Aβ. It indicates how strongly the feedback loop acts. Large loop gain makes closed-loop gain approach 1/β, but stability still must be verified.

6. Does negative feedback always improve stability?

It improves gain stability against parameter variation, but it does not guarantee dynamic stability. Extra phase shift can turn nominal negative feedback into positive feedback near a loop crossover frequency.

7. How do you determine the gain of a non-inverting op-amp?

For an ideal op-amp, V− ≈ V+. The divider gives V− = VoR1/(R1 + Rf) = Vin, so gain = 1 + Rf/R1.

8. The measured gain is lower than expected. What do you check?

Check resistor values and connections, source/load impedance, supply rails, output clipping, op-amp gain-bandwidth product, frequency, probe setup and whether the device is stable with the load.

9. What is gain desensitivity?

It is the reduction of closed-loop gain sensitivity to open-loop gain variation. The sensitivity is 1/(1 + Aβ).

10. How would you verify the circuit in Multisim?

Run transient analysis with a small sine input, measure input and output amplitudes and phase, then run AC analysis for magnitude and phase. Compare passband gain to the resistor-ratio prediction and inspect bandwidth and clipping.

11. What practical factors limit the ideal result?

Finite open-loop gain, finite GBW, slew rate, output swing, input offset voltage, input bias current, noise, resistor tolerance, loading and temperature drift.

12. What is phase margin and why does it matter?

Phase margin is the difference between −180° and the loop phase at unity loop-gain crossover for the usual negative-feedback convention. Adequate phase margin reduces ringing and oscillation risk.

Industry perspective

Voltage-series feedback is fundamental to precision voltage amplifiers, sensor-conditioning stages, active filters, instrumentation front ends and buffer/driver circuits. In product design, engineers choose the feedback network while checking noise gain, bandwidth, stability with capacitive loads, output drive, supply range, accuracy over temperature and manufacturability.

9. Applications and industry relevance

Sensor signal conditioning

Amplifies small sensor voltages while presenting a high input resistance to avoid loading the sensor.

Non-inverting op-amp stages

Provides a defined voltage gain while preserving input polarity.

Active filters

Op-amp feedback networks set filter gain and participate in frequency-selective behaviour.

Voltage buffers and drivers

Feedback helps achieve low output resistance for driving following stages.

Engineering considerations

  • Choose an op-amp with sufficient gain-bandwidth product and slew rate.
  • Verify output voltage swing and output current for the intended load.
  • Check stability with the real PCB layout and capacitive loads.
  • Use resistor tolerances and temperature coefficients appropriate to gain accuracy.
  • Consider input-referred noise, offset and bias-current errors for low-level signals.

Career relevance

Useful knowledge for analog design, mixed-signal design, hardware validation, electronics R&D, semiconductor applications engineering, test engineering and embedded hardware roles.

High-priority GATE and interview points

  • Remember the pair: series mixing → input resistance increases; voltage sampling → output resistance decreases.
  • For negative feedback, denominator is 1 + Aβ.
  • For Aβ ≫ 1, gain approaches 1/β.
  • Non-inverting op-amp is a standard practical voltage-series example.
  • Distinguish gain stability from loop stability.
  • Check phase margin, bandwidth, slew rate and output swing in practical designs.
